# Click: verified book method and coverage boundaries

- The supplied book supports a Foundation Sprint that ends with a **testable Founding Hypothesis**, followed by experiments; finishing its worksheets does not establish product-market fit.
- This review adds procedural detail, chart contents, safeguards, testing advice, and explicit scope limits to the [public-source baseline](knapp-zeratsky-baseline.md).
- This is source research for later playbooks, not a finished facilitator guide or a decision about the mentor room's design.

## Foundation Sprint activity inventory

Stable `CLICK-*` identifiers are **our reference labels**, not the authors' numbering. Page references identify the source-stated procedure and outputs. Where chapter prose and checklist differ, both are recorded below.

| ID | Source-stated activity and resulting output | PDF pages |
|---|---|---|
| `CLICK-SETUP` | Recruit the actual project Decider; assemble at most five people with contrasting perspectives; choose a Facilitator. Reserve two days, clear competing commitments, prepare shared workspace, supplies and individual workbooks as applicable. Introduce roles, the process, independent work, and a provisional rather than perfect result. | 31-34, 170-172 |
| `CLICK-NOTE-VOTE` | Ask a clear question; give about five minutes for anonymous individual answers, one per note; display them; silently review and cast two or three votes; permit brief debate if the Decider needs it; let the Decider choose without being bound by votes. Output: a selected proposal. | 42-46, 172 |
| `CLICK-CUSTOMER` | Choose one target customer using Note-and-Vote. Use plain language and a common-sense check that identifies real people. | 42-47, 172 |
| `CLICK-PROBLEM` | Repeat for one customer problem statement; check that it describes an actual problem rather than a desired feature disguised as a problem. | 46-47, 173 |
| `CLICK-ADVANTAGES` | Independently propose capabilities, insights, and motivations; Decider selects one advantage in each category. Identify what the team can unusually deliver, understands about customers/problem, and specifically cares about. | 52-58, 173 |
| `CLICK-COMPETITION` | Consider direct products, substitutes/workarounds, and doing nothing. Use known alternatives, modest research, or ask customers how they solve the problem today. Checklist selects three to five competitors and identifies the toughest alternative. Consolidate customer, problem, three advantages, and competition into the **Basics** sheet. | 62-66, 173-174 |
| `CLICK-DIFFERENTIATORS` | Imagine the best attainable outcome with the actual team. Individually place dots on classic scales; generate custom positive/opposite scales and score those too. Everyone silently votes for six preferred differentiators; Decider chooses two to try. Output: candidate scales and initial selected pair. | 81-84, 175-176 |
| `CLICK-DIFFERENTIATION-CHART` | Plot the project and strongest competitors on a 2x2 using the selected pair. Iterate factors until the project occupies the upper-right quadrant alone, while honestly representing competitors and deliverable promises. Focus on customer perception. Output: a provisional differentiation chart. | 84-86, 176 |
| `CLICK-PRINCIPLES` | Note-and-Vote practical project advice. Suggested formula: one principle per differentiator plus one protecting customers from harmful success. Decider chooses approximately three; limit group wordsmithing to five minutes. Combine principles and chart into the **Mini Manifesto**. | 92-98, 176-178 |
| `CLICK-OPTIONS` | Generate alternatives to the first approach, including known ideas, responses to failure, imagined competitors' approaches, and different target customers. Decider selects options to compare. Output: multiple plausible approaches, not a commitment to the original idea. | 107-110, 179 |
| `CLICK-OPTION-SUMMARIES` | Divide the selected approaches among participants. Each gets one page: title, one sentence explaining its value with reference to customer problem/differentiation, and a quick explanatory doodle. Doodle time is at most five minutes. | 110-114, 179-180 |
| `CLICK-LENS-CRITERIA` | Give approaches distinct letters/colors. Silently read summaries and attach one to four positive criteria to each, such as ease of building or audience size. Output: identifiable options and an explicit criteria pool. | 181 |
| `CLICK-CLASSIC-LENSES` | Create customer, pragmatic, growth, and money charts. Plot each option, preferably one axis at a time, using an expert or Decider for relative placement. Labels may change to fit the project. Output: comparable views of the same options. | 121-127, 182 |
| `CLICK-CUSTOM-LENSES` | Silently revisit/add criteria, vote, and have the Decider select two to start a custom 2x2. Plot, adjust, name, and repeat. Also plot approaches on the differentiation chart. Output: additional project-specific comparisons. | 126-127, 183 |
| `CLICK-APPROACH-DECISION` | Review all charts for consistent winners, contradictory views, or one especially useful lens. Decider chooses **one top bet and one backup**. This is judgment informed by visible tradeoffs, not a mandated arithmetic total. | 127-128, 183 |
| `CLICK-HYPOTHESIS` | Combine customer, problem, approach, competition, and differentiation into one conditional claim: helping these people solve this problem this way should make them choose it over alternatives for these reasons. Output: **Founding Hypothesis** and its scorecard questions. | 135, 138-141, 183-184 |

**Chart content recovered visually:** the eight classic differentiation dimensions concern speed, ease, price, focus, simplicity, integration, automation, and intelligence (p. 83). Default lens axes are: customer = problem-solving quality/ease of use; pragmatic = build speed/build cost; growth = potential customer count/ease of adoption; money = potential customer count/long-term value (p. 182). These are starting points, not measured financial or market facts.

**Counts differ within this edition:** options chapter recommends three or more, narrowing if there are more than five (pp. 107, 110); checklist selects **three to seven** (p. 179). Chapter suggests **one to three custom lenses**, with differentiation as another possible lens (p. 127); checklist aims for **two to four additional charts**, then separately tries the differentiation chart (p. 183). Do not silently merge these into one purportedly exact recipe. The inventory keeps both; a later playbook must identify its chosen source/convention.

## Experiments begin after the Foundation Sprint

The scorecard asks whether the customer, problem, approach, and differentiation are right; whether customers would switch; and whether the solution clicks. Its checkboxes are prompts for investigation, not a numeric validation threshold (pp. 140-141, 184).

| ID | Source-stated follow-through and output | PDF pages |
|---|---|---|
| `CLICK-TEST-FOCUS` | List important project risks and select the crucial test target. Consider testing the discovery/marketing/sales story first to examine customer, problem, differentiation, and willingness to take a next step. Output: risk-focused experiment and prototype scope. | 146, 157 |
| `CLICK-DESIGN-SPRINT` | Recommended five-day sequence: map the customer problem; sketch competing solutions; decide what to test; make realistic prototypes; test with customers. Output: customer observations of a realistic simulated experience. Full operational instructions are deferred to *Sprint* and its guide. | 146-156 |
| `CLICK-COMPARATIVE-TESTS` | Consider multiple prototypes head-to-head; also compare with actual competitors, substitutes, or the status quo. Output: observed choices and reactions under comparison. The Slack story uses separate fictitious brands for competing prototypes. | 157-158 |
| `CLICK-LEARN-REPEAT` | Review individual hypothesis elements; retain promising elements, revise others, and retest. Watch behavior and unguarded reactions, including willingness to spend time/money, rather than treating advice as equivalent evidence. Output: increased or decreased confidence and an adjusted solution/hypothesis. | 149-151, 158-160 |

The Phaidra example tests **four engineers per round**, twice (pp. 149-150); this is a case description, not a replacement for the public Design Sprint guide's five-customer recommendation. The second prototype retained the customer/problem/approach while changing how the dashboard delivered transparency. This is source support for learning at the **hypothesis-element** level, not for an invented universal pass/fail test.

## Completion, applicability, and evidence limits

The formal Foundation Sprint ends after producing the hypothesis, ready for tests (pp. 169, 183). It aims at a rough, immediately testable draft (p. 34), not a finished implementation plan. The preferred wider program is a Foundation Sprint plus three Design Sprints across four weeks (p. 18); even after that month some teams are still revising differentiation (p. 163).

The book encourages testing real commitments and reading reactions, but **explicitly acknowledges that prototype interviews are simulations and do not establish live-market product-market fit** (pp. 159-160). The reviewed procedure provides no numerical confidence rule, universal revenue threshold, statistical guarantee, or fixed number of loops that proves an idea worth pursuing. Its claims of effectiveness draw on practitioner experience and case stories, not a causal evaluation of this entire process (pp. 163-166).

Scope includes organizations beyond startups and projects already underway. A Foundation Sprint can stand alone only if another way to test its hypothesis follows. Basics homework may enable a one-day workshop. The first day alone can serve differentiation; the second can support a complex choice, but the authors caution that the days are designed to work together. Solo and remote work are allowed; a real team is preferred, and a team sprint requires the actual Decider (pp. 185-188). The recommendation to try the original recipe before modifying it is source advice, not a restriction on this user's authorized research or later adaptation (p. 186).

## Deltas and open mentor-room implications

Compared with the [baseline](knapp-zeratsky-baseline.md), substantive book coverage now establishes the **safeguard principle**, precise output fields, criteria preparation and plotting mechanics, chart defaults, selection-count discrepancies, alternative-customer options, story-first/comparative tests, and the authors' own PMF limitation. It does not close other-source gaps.

**Our proposed implications, not author instructions or settled architecture:** use these IDs when reconciling mandatory Knapp/Zeratsky coverage; consider every other expert and route relevant sessions as the user requested. Check prior work against each output's purpose and current scope before crediting it. Keep evaluated hypotheses and evidence scoped to one idea with its own history; preserve promising components for later separate evaluation without transferring whole-product validation to them. The book's Reclaim feature-selection example (pp. 124-127) and Phaidra's selective revision (pp. 149-151) make these useful design questions, but do not specify a component ledger, file layout, AI mentor format, or session length.

Charts may expose a need for technical, distribution, or economic expertise; completing those charts does not complete those investigations. Treat AI viewpoints as aids to reasoning, not independent experts or customers. The exact completion checks, evidence-credit rules, conversational format, and investment decision standard remain to be designed and tested.
