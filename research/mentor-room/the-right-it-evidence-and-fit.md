# The Right It: evidence, decisions, and fit with the mentor room

- Savoia adds a concrete discipline for testing **market engagement before substantial investment**, with explicit hypotheses, meaningful commitments, repeated experiments, and decisions tied to observed results.
- His scores, probability labels, and experiment counts are practitioner heuristics. They do not certify success, replace other discovery questions, or turn fictional teaching examples into observed evidence.

## Source-stated practices and outputs

`RIGHTIT-EV-*` IDs are our stable reference labels, not names of author-supplied worksheets or mandatory mentor sessions.

| ID | Source-stated practice → useful output | Locator |
|---|---|---|
| `RIGHTIT-EV-SUCCESS` | Define the result expected from the proposed investment before proceeding. Success meets or exceeds that result; failure falls short. Different stakeholders can judge the same outcome differently. → Explicit success criteria. | Ch. 1, **Market Failure and Success Defined**, [16–18](derived/the-right-it/009-chapter-1.md#_idParaDest-5c). |
| `RIGHTIT-EV-FLOP` | Examine Launch, Operations, and Premise: did intended customers encounter/access it; did execution meet expectations; did they actually want it? → A differentiated failure explanation. | `RIGHTIT-EV-CALIPER` | Classify target-market responses by something of value committed; customize relative point values to the product and market. → An account of commitment strength, rather than a count of compliments. | Ch. 6, **The Skin-in-the-Game Caliper**, [149–158](derived/the-right-it/015-chapter-6.md#_idParaDest-17a). |
| `RIGHTIT-EV-TRI` | Compare each experiment's data with its specific hypothesis; place a separate arrow on a five-category meter. → A visual interpretation of support, shortfall, or ambiguity. | Ch. 6, **The TRI Meter**, [158–165](derived/the-right-it/015-chapter-6.md#_idParaDest-17b). |
| `RIGHTIT-EV-REPLICATE` | Test multiple related hypotheses through multiple experiments; consider investment, affordable loss, desired confidence, and result clarity. → A broader evidence set. | `RIGHTIT-EV-LOCAL` | Start with an accessible subset of the target market; locality can mean geography, technical platform, or an online community. | `RIGHTIT-EV-FAST-CHEAP` | After framing the hypothesis, seek a faster, cheaper experiment without sacrificing data quality; compare alternatives rather than accepting the first test design. → Hours to Data and Dollars to Data estimates. | Ch. 7, **Testing Now Beats Testing Later** and **Think Cheap, Cheaper, Cheapest**, [181–186](derived/the-right-it/017-chapter-7.md#_idParaDest-21b). |

## Numeric tools require careful interpretation

**The Law of Market Failure is a deliberate rhetorical framing.** Chapter 1 reports approximately 80% disappointing/failed/canceled consumer launches from Nielsen, mentions a 70–90% range, and recommends assuming 90% failure. These are the author's reported statistics and cautionary starting assumption, not a measured base rate for this user's software idea. Its footnote explicitly admits that calling it a law is a stretch and that “observation” or “rule of thumb” would be alternatives. The Success Equation and its `2^n − 1` failing binary combinations illustrate multiple necessary factors; counting combinations alone does not establish their probabilities. ([Statistics/equation, 18–20](derived/the-right-it/009-chapter-1.md#_idParaDest-5d); [footnote](derived/the-right-it/027-ch1fn1.md#rch1f1).)

**Right It is conditional, not a personal guarantee.** The definition assumes competent execution and market success; the next paragraph denies guaranteed success for the particular founder because execution and competition still matter. The concluding “80ish percent” improvement and eventual-success promises are the author's expectations, qualified by experiment quality, competent execution, external events, and learning. The reviewed material supplies no calibrated predictive study supporting those numbers for an individual project. ([Definition, 29–30](derived/the-right-it/010-chapter-2.md#_idParaDest-7); [promises, 232–233](derived/the-right-it/019-chapter-9.md#page_232).)

**Caliper points are relative, customizable judgments.** The Tortell-o-matic example assigns zero to opinions, likes, and hypothetical-purchase surveys; one to a validated, knowingly supplied email; ten to a validated phone number; one per minute of committed time; and one per dollar. Savoia recommends orders of magnitude rather than false precision. Purchased/unconsented email lists count zero. These values are neither conversion probabilities nor a universal total required to proceed. Preserve what was actually offered, committed, paid, attended, delivered, and refunded; a founder's own sunk cost is not evidence of customer demand. ([Caliper example, 153–158](derived/the-right-it/015-chapter-6.md#page_153); [consent footnote](derived/the-right-it/036-ch6-footnote-2.md#rch6_fn2).)

**The TRI Meter is an interpretive display.** Its visual labels are approximately 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90%. The mapping places substantial outperformance at Very Likely, meeting/slightly exceeding the hypothesis at Likely, modest shortfall at Unlikely, and severe shortfall at Very Unlikely. Ambiguous/confounded data goes to 50/50 or is discarded. In this use, 50/50 can mean **uninterpretable**, not a measured equal chance. The chapter supplies no formal calibration, uncertainty calculation, or rule for combining arrows into one posterior probability. Keep any future display categorical and explicitly heuristic. ([Mapping, 159–165](derived/the-right-it/015-chapter-6.md#page_159); [inspected scale](derived/the-right-it/images/009-image159.jpg).)

**Counts are guidance, not certificates.** Preserve the three-to-five-experiment recommendation as Savoia's advice; do not confuse it with three-to-five participants or independent replications. The book's twelve-experiment/five-version sequence gives each version its own arrows; it does not pool failed versions into the final version's support. More tests must answer meaningful remaining questions. Neither repeating the same biased test nor importing multiple mentors' interpretations creates independent evidence. ([Experiment count](derived/the-right-it/015-chapter-6.md#page_167); [version sequence](derived/the-right-it/015-chapter-6.md#page_172).)

## BusU teaches iteration but supplies no real customer results

Chapter 8 **explicitly labels BusU a fictional scenario**, inspired by an actual idea. The 600-person sample, 132 purchasers, 48% repeat figure, revenue, refunds, teaching volunteers, and customer comments are invented teaching outcomes. They must never enter an evidence ledger as a real business case or validated benchmark. The assertion that 100 representative people is statistically adequate is also case narration, not a general sample-size rule. ([Fiction disclosure, 219–220](derived/the-right-it/018-chapter-8.md#_idParaDest-23e); [sample assertion, 207](derived/the-right-it/018-chapter-8.md#page_207).)

The useful pattern is increasingly consequential tests: an email signup, attendance, paid trial, then repeat purchase. The first offer omits price; later clarifying $3,000 tuition and no employer reimbursement reduces signups. The author then proposes changing the threshold, price, or course format. **Proposed adaptation:** preserve the original hypothesis and its shortfall, create a new version with a rationale, and test it prospectively. A signup after seeing a price still records a signup, not actual payment. The source's favorable interpretation of the confounded first result and threshold adjustment should remain visible rather than silently becoming clean validation. ([Offer changes, 208–212](derived/the-right-it/018-chapter-8.md#page_208).)

Similarly, the shorter course's supposed success does not establish demand for the longer course. Morning/evening feedback reveals an experience issue, and refunds reveal delivery/economic implications. These illustrate learning about different parts of a proposition. Preserve actual repeat purchases separately from stated preferences—the narrative calls both YODA, despite its stricter skin-in-the-game definition. ([Results and next steps, 215–218](derived/the-right-it/018-chapter-8.md#page_215).)

## Proposed integration, distinct from author instructions

**Use the right evidence for the question.** Savoia's critique targets opinions as predictions of future demand. Chapter 2 nevertheless acknowledges useful insights from research, and BusU uses comments to diagnose friction. The room should retain interviews about past behavior, needs, and usability; their lack of purchase commitment does not make them useless for those questions. Preserve YODA's narrower meaning when invoking his method. Neither an agent simulation nor a mentor's approval supplies real customer commitment. ([Thoughtland limits, 36–42](derived/the-right-it/010-chapter-2.md#page_36); [existing evidence distinctions](evidence-and-overlap.md#verified-methods-serve-different-decisions).)

**Demand does not settle every risk.** The glossary's suggestion that a market usually enables a way around engineering, financial, or legal obstacles is optimistic guidance, not evidence that those obstacles are solved. FLOP itself distinguishes launch and operations. Track value, usability, technical delivery, economics, and organizational constraints separately; reject the inference that deposits alone make a product feasible or viable. Likewise, Savoia's narrow prototype/pretotype vocabulary should not redefine Design Sprint or Pattern Breakers prototypes, which can investigate desirability. ([Glossary, 250](derived/the-right-it/021-glossary.md#page_250); [prototype distinction, 227–228](derived/the-right-it/019-chapter-9.md#page_227); [existing four-risk mapping](evidence-and-overlap.md#verified-methods-serve-different-decisions).)

**Complement the mandatory Knapp–Zeratsky work.** A Founding Hypothesis can yield a measurable engagement claim; a relevant prototype can serve both a Sprint question and a pretotype experiment. Credit the shared artifact once while preserving each unanswered question. Click already encourages real commitments and explicitly limits prototype interviews' PMF implications; Savoia sharpens action specificity and subsequent field testing. His preference for rapid testing does not cancel this user's mandatory core or authorize replacing it with a signup page. ([Click experiments](click-book-method.md#experiments-begin-after-the-foundation-sprint); [Sprint baseline](knapp-zeratsky-baseline.md#design-sprint-produces-observed-customer-reactions).)

**Preserve components and disconfirmation.** Pincus's atomic MIS and Pattern Breakers' implementation/audience/insight distinction complement Savoia's tweaking. Reuse an observation only when proposition, audience, context, date, and action match. Preserve a promising component of a rejected combination without transferring its demand claim to a standalone product. Changing audience must have a recorded reason, not merely remove negative responses. ([Pincus](pincus-idea-methods.md#proposed-mentor-room-implications--not-settled-architecture); [Pattern Breakers](pattern-breakers-evidence-and-fit.md#fit-and-tensions-proposed-synthesis-for-the-skill).)

**Match effort and ambition to this idea.** The book expressly includes features and noncommercial initiatives; “market” can mean people adopting or using an idea. Its global language and VC example do not require venture ambition. For a personal tool, define useful behavior and affordable effort; for a business, add buyer and operating economics. DTD/HTD/$TD compare feasible tests, while the 48-hour teaching limit is contextual. Keep each idea's independent history, audit existing work for credit, and consider every expert while explaining omissions. These are the user's requirements and our proposed synthesis, not a completed skill architecture. ([Source scope, 5–6](derived/the-right-it/007-book-overview.md#page_5); [HTD, 183–184](derived/the-right-it/017-chapter-7.md#page_183).)
