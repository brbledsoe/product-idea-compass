## Preserve the need; compare implementations

Underlying need: manage a distant rental with fewer interruptions and confidence that necessary work happens. Its prevalence and commercial importance beyond the family remain hypotheses.

| Approach | Customer and offer | Rough flow | Main attraction | Main uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Simple all-in-one host app | Independent owner gets one place for booking-linked chores, access, messages, and property status | Booking → automated tasks/access/messages → owner status | Coherent experience, subscription model | Large existing feature overlap; migration and integration work; whether simplicity is noticeably better |
| B. Coordinate the owner's existing providers | Remote owner connects trusted people and gets follow-through on cleaning and maintenance | Task → primary accepts → backup if needed → completion evidence → escalation | Narrower build scope and no initial need to create a provider marketplace | Existing tools already do much of this; provider participation, exception handling, and willingness to pay |
| C. Find providers and own fulfillment | Owner requests a result and the platform sources local capacity and resolves failures | Incident → provider match → dispatch → quality check → recovery | Closest to the desired management-agency outcome | Local supply, quality, urgency, disputes, and human operating costs; risks becoming a managed service |
| D. Software for local co-hosts/service coordinators | A local operator uses software to serve multiple remote owners with less admin | Portfolio queue → provider coordination → owner reports | Existing operator supplies local knowledge and responsibility; several properties per buyer | Different buyer and sales process; competition from established operations software |

Count: four meaningful variants, consistent with the pinned preferred Foundation range. These short summaries are not full option sheets or the required classic/custom charts. No top bet or backup has been chosen by the human Decider.

**Facilitator's initial learning preference:** investigate B against a concrete incident; retain D as an alternative business configuration. A remains possible if real usability failures emerge. C needs evidence of local supply and a sustainable responsibility model before a low-fee promise. This is provisional judgment, not a completed approach decision.

## Components that can fail independently

| Component | Proposed value | What would weaken it? | Possible alternative context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cleaning/backup coordination | Less chasing and fewer uncovered turnovers | Hosts already get sufficient coverage from Turno/current cleaner, or backups are unavailable | Co-host/cleaning-team operations |
| Repair triage and follow-through | Convert reported issue into approved, accepted, completed work | Diagnostics/pricing require repeated founder intervention, or jobs are too rare to support a subscription | Local maintenance coordinator tooling |
| Access automation | Correct provider/guest access at the right time | Native integrations already suffice, or hardware setup/support dominates value | Hardware-specific integration product |
| Inspection and damage evidence | Help a person identify and document issues between stays | Inconsistent/missing photos or poor accuracy creates more review work | Cleaner checklist/inspection workflow |
| Routine communication and reviews | Reduce repetitive host administration | Existing/native automation is sufficient | Integration or supporting feature |
| Distribution and local-event promotion | Reach relevant guests across channels | Acquisition effort or permissions outweigh incremental bookings | Separate marketing tool or service |

All components are commercially untested. A report/photo can trigger human review; it does not establish who caused damage or whether a charge is justified. Current app capability is unknown and receives no technical validation credit.

## Two-programmer operating boundary to test

A narrow software product is plausible given the team's reported capabilities, but reliability and commercial feasibility have not been measured. The critical dependency is the availability and cooperation of local people.

An illustrative experience for B:

1. A booking change or provider report creates a task with deadline and owner-approved rules.
2. An approved provider accepts; no acceptance or cancellation triggers the agreed backup process.
3. Access is arranged through an authorized, compatible integration.
4. Completion is recorded with a checklist or requested photos; unclear evidence stays unresolved.
5. The owner sees confirmed work and decisions requiring attention. An unresolved issue goes to a named person, not an empty automated queue.

AI could assist with message interpretation, translation, summaries, and collecting missing information. Explicit rules should govern deadlines, acceptance, spending limits, and escalation. Whether these interfaces and data are available must be tested for the actual systems. This is an architecture hypothesis, not an inspected implementation.

If the company promises that a person will show up and fix any problem, it must arrange the remaining human response. A low software fee must be tested against that operating burden. Contractor work remains an expense even if coordination becomes cheaper.

## Proposed learning sequence

**First: reconstruct one real incident and finish Foundation Basics.** Use the existing app and current workaround as evidence inputs. Separate problem frequency, provider availability, software friction, and trust. Choose a concrete customer and next investment with Brandon. No new product build is necessary for this step.

**Then: compare actual alternatives and observe target hosts.** Recruit hosts in one reachable region, including people already using competing software. Ask about their last incidents and inspect what they actually did. Use the required Design Sprint work to compare realistic approaches with five screened target customers; recruitment, prototype, study, and observations remain pending. The recommended three iteration cycles remain in the core and have not been completed or waived.

**If that reveals a gap: specify a small paid operating pilot.** A possible learning scope is 3–5 non-family hosts in one region using existing approved providers, for a defined period long enough to observe multiple turnovers. These counts and the sequence are facilitator proposals, not author-prescribed thresholds. Rare repairs may need a longer observation window. Do not interpret no incidents as proof of exception handling.

Before a pilot starts, agree and record:

- Exact fee, included tasks, excluded costs, and who responds when all providers decline.
- A denominator and time window for owner interventions, completed eligible tasks, unfilled requests, founder minutes, and repeat paid use.
- Baseline measurement and success/failure/unclear criteria, grounded in the founders' desired economics and the host's meaningful time savings.
- Honest disclosure of any manual work; manually performed coordination tests demand and operations, not autonomous software feasibility.

No thresholds are preregistered yet. No contacts have been recruited, no offer sent, no payment collected, and no pilot launched. Family experience remains distinct from evidence of independent demand.

## Economics to measure

Monthly contribution per property = subscription/service revenue minus software/API/messaging/payment costs minus human support/coordination labor minus company-borne service-recovery costs. Onboarding cost and customer acquisition also need recovery over the actual customer lifetime. Do not count founder labor as free.

An agency fee comparison must compare equivalent scope. It is not reasonable to count a manager's entire fee as savings while transferring the manager's remaining work back to the owner. Real owner alternatives, regional provider prices, and proposed fee are still unknown.

## Recommended mentor sequence

1. **Knapp–Zeratsky Foundation:** customer, concrete problem, competition, differentiation, and meaningful alternative approaches.
2. **Pincus:** isolate the smallest valuable component and prepare an honest Minimum Idea State after choosing the risk.
3. **Savoia + Bland–Osterwalder:** define a measurable commitment test and explicit Test Card; apply their saved methods when preparing the experiment. These exercises were selected, not performed today.
4. **Knapp–Zeratsky Design Sprint and iteration:** realistic comparison with actual screened hosts, retaining observations, contradictions, and cycle decisions. Add live operational/payment tests for claims a prototype cannot establish.
